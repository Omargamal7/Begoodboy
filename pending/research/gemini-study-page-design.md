# Evidence-Based Web Design Guide for a Grade 6 Autistic Learner in an Egyptian Inclusive Language School

> Gemini Deep Research, 17 November 2026. This is the report as delivered, including its figure, recommendations, evidence labels, 51 checklist items and bibliography. The [research index](README.md) records which guidance the repository currently uses.

Designing interactive study and self-quizzing web pages for a Grade 6 student (approximately 11–12 years old) with high-functioning Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) in an Egyptian language school requires aligning three distinct bodies of knowledge: empirical autism intervention research, international web cognitive accessibility standards, and Egyptian inclusive education regulations<sup>[1]</sup>. Because the student attends an Egyptian language school with an inclusion division (*نظام* *الدمج* *التعليمي*), his core subjects (such as Science, Mathematics, and English) are taught in a second language, while his formal school assessments are governed by **Egyptian Ministry of Education and Technical Education (MoETE) Ministerial Decree No. 252 of 2017** (and subsequent updates through Circular 9 of 2024)<sup>[3]</sup>. Under Decree 252 of 2017, integrated autistic students with an IQ above 65 are evaluated **100% through objective questions** (*الامتحانات* *الموضوعية*)—principally Multiple-Choice Questions (MCQs) and True/False items—printed in **18-point font** with questions and answer spaces integrated on a single sheet rather than separated onto bubble sheets<sup>[3]</sup>.

Every design rule in this guide is rated strictly by the strength of its underlying primary evidence so that consensus standards and exploratory findings are never conflated with clinical proof:

- **EVIDENCE-BASED**: Supported directly by peer-reviewed empirical studies, randomized controlled trials (RCTs), single-case experimental designs (SCDs), or meta-analyses conducted specifically with autistic children and adolescents, including the **National Clearinghouse on Autism Evidence and Practice (NCAEP) 2020 Third-Generation Review** (*Steinbrenner et al., 2020; Hume et al., 2021*, which remains the current, most comprehensive systematic review of 972 autism intervention studies)<sup>[5]</sup> and a **2024 meta-analysis of 33 RCTs of digital interventions in ASD** showing a large overall effect size (Cohen's d = 1.89 vs. all controls, though smaller at d = 0.73 against active controls)<sup>[8]</sup>.

- **CONSENSUS**: Supported by authoritative accessibility and instructional standards—specifically **W3C WCAG 2.2** (*December 2024 W3C Recommendation*, the final normative edition of WCAG 2)<sup>[10]</sup>, **W3C COGA "Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities"** (*April 2021 Working Group Note*, which remains the current normative W3C cognitive design guide, supplemented by the *February 2026 W3C Cognitive Accessibility Research Modules Draft*)<sup>[1]</sup>, **CAST Universal Design for Learning (UDL) Guidelines 3.0** (*released July 2024*, current edition)<sup>[14]</sup>, **UK Government accessibility guidance** (*GOV.UK Home Office Autism Poster* and *DfE COGA Manual*)<sup>[16]</sup>, **Egyptian MoETE Decree 252/2017 specifications**<sup>[3]</sup>, or empirical psychometric and multimedia research conducted on broader neurodivergent or second-language student populations (such as Mayer's segmenting principle at d = 1.36, coherence/weeding at d = 0.90, spatial contiguity at d = 0.48, synchronized text-plus-audio for second-language learners at d = 0.57, and text-to-speech comprehension gains at d = 0.35)<sup>[18]</sup>.

- **WEAK**: Supported only by small-sample pilot studies, qualitative case observations, unvalidated heuristic proposals, or mixed empirical findings where direct evidence for academic web interfaces in autism is thin<sup>[22]</sup>.

Two areas in this guide exhibit thin direct evidence, which is an expected and honest reflection of the current literature. First, regarding **Minecraft-style themes**, while embedding a child's special interest into instruction as an antecedent scaffold is EVIDENCE-BASED<sup>[24]</sup>, peer-reviewed studies specifically testing *Minecraft* with autistic pre-teens (such as a February 2026 pilot trial with 10–12-year-olds) evaluate playing the 3D game itself for social collaboration or sensory self-regulation rather than applying Minecraft visual styling to academic web pages<sup>[22]</sup>; therefore, Minecraft UI styling rules are rated CONSENSUS or WEAK. Second, regarding **Egyptian MoETE guidance**, full technical exam-paper specifications under Ministerial Decree No. 252 of 2017 are published primarily in Arabic administrative circulars rather than English online portals<sup>[3]</sup>, though core parameters (100% objective format, 18pt font, single-sheet layout, and second-foreign-language exemption) are verified via both Arabic ministerial texts and a March 2025 British Council and MoETE policy review<sup>[3]</sup>. Furthermore, across all topics, quantitative evidence highlights why strict linguistic and visual discipline is essential: autistic learners face a baseline figurative language comprehension gap (Hedges' g = -0.57) and incur a 985 ms reaction-time penalty when processing novel metaphors compared to 198 ms in neurotypical controls (d = 1.05)<sup>[31]</sup>.

## 1. Layout and Predictability

Autistic learners exhibit heightened sensitivity to visual complexity and unexpected layout changes; eye-tracking and usability studies indicate that inconsistent scanpaths, unbordered sections, and shifting controls induce anxiety and rapid task abandonment<sup>[17]</sup>. Furthermore, because the student's formal Egyptian school exams under Ministerial Decree No. 252 of 2017 use a single-sheet layout where questions and answers appear together in 18-point type<sup>[3]</sup>, his interactive web pages should mirror that exact structural predictability so that study habits transfer seamlessly to school exam conditions<sup>[36]</sup>. In practice, every page should follow a uniform spatial blueprint: a predictable single-column container on a soft off-white, cream, or pale sage-gray background enclosed by clean dark-slate or warm earthy borders<sup>[34]</sup>. In the lesson view (displaying one concept per screen), a top bar shows the lesson title and a block-style progress tracker (e.g., "Progress: Block 2 of 4"), while the bordered central card places a sharp instructional diagram on the left and 2–3 bullet points of 18pt ragged-right literal text on the right, accompanied by a consistent "Listen to Page (1.0x)" audio bar and descriptive bottom navigation buttons ("Back to Block 1" and "Go to Quiz Question 1")<sup>[1]</sup>. When transitioning to the quiz view, the outer header, progress bar, and footer shell remain in the exact same coordinates, while the central box presents one 3-option MCQ or True/False item at a time with explicit radio-button instructions ("Choose one option") and neutral gray strike-through exclusion if a wrong option is selected<sup>[16]</sup>.

## 2. Language (Literal, Short)

Meta-analytic and eye-tracking evidence indicates that figurative language imposes a heavy cognitive tax on autistic learners. Across 41 studies (45 effect sizes), autistic individuals show poorer comprehension of figurative language overall compared to neurotypical peers (Hedges' g = -0.57), with metaphors posing a sharper barrier than irony or sarcasm (g = -0.63 across 14 metaphor studies) and yielding 7.5-fold lower odds of accurate comprehension than matched literal statements<sup>[32]</sup>. Even when linguistically able autistic readers successfully decode a metaphor, a 2025 eye-tracking and reaction-time study demonstrated that their processing cost averages **985 ms** per metaphor compared to **198 ms** for neurotypical controls (Cohen's d = 1.05)—an ~800 ms cognitive delay per figurative phrase that drains working memory needed for Grade 6 academic concepts<sup>[31]</sup>.

## 3. Visuals and Dual Coding

Visual Supports and Technology-Aided Instruction and Intervention (TAII) are both classified as **Evidence-Based Practices** in the NCAEP 2020 systematic review<sup>[24]</sup>. Autistic children demonstrate strong visual cognition advantages<sup>[51]</sup>, and pairing text with structured visual representations—such as graphic organizers, concept maps, and clear diagrams—compensates for executive function and central coherence challenges during reading comprehension<sup>[52]</sup>. However, studies of autistic interfaces show that **low-to-moderate visual fidelity** (clean vector diagrams, clear schematic drawings, or sharp, isolated photos) outperforms cluttered, photorealistic, or overlapping graphics<sup>[39]</sup>.

## 4. Colour

Empirical research on colour perception in autism shows that sensory hyper-reactivity alters visual comfort<sup>[58]</sup>. In a clinical comparison of 29 boys with ASD aged 4–17 (including an 11–17-year-old subgroup) against 38 age-matched neurotypical boys across six colours, **Grandgeorge & Masataka (2016)** found that autistic boys were **significantly less likely to prefer yellow**—which the authors attribute to visual hyper-sensation making high-luminance yellow sensory-overloading—and **significantly more likely to prefer green and brown**<sup>[58]</sup>. Fortunately, green and brown are the foundational earthy tones of *Minecraft* (grass blocks, oak wood, stone), making a muted, low-saturation earthy palette naturally aligned with both clinical autism colour research and the student's preferred theme<sup>[38]</sup>.

## 5. Motion and Sound

## 6. Feedback After Wrong Answers

How a learning interface responds to an incorrect answer is critical for an autistic Grade 6 student. Two distinct findings from autism research must be synthesized here:

- **Non-Punitive, Non-Sensory Error Handling**: In co-design and RCT evaluations of educational software for autistic children, **Fletcher-Watson et al. (2016)** established that interfaces should **avoid punitive feedback** (no harsh buzzers, red flashing screens, sad faces, or point deductions), and avoid playing novel sound effects or funny animations on wrong answers—because some autistic children find novel error effects rewarding and may **choose wrong answers repeatedly** just to trigger the reaction<sup>[66]</sup>.

- **Explicit Error Correction with Option Exclusion**: In a Randomized Clinical Trial (N = 28) comparing **Error Correction** against **Errorless Learning** (most-to-least prompting) in autistic children with strong vocabulary scores (standard score > 75, matching a high-functioning Grade 6 profile), **Leaf et al. (2020)** found that **Error Correction produced significantly more independent correct responses** (Mann-Whitney U = 53, p = 0.039) than errorless learning, with near-zero frustration behaviors<sup>[72]</sup>. Similarly, **Direct Instruction (DI)**—an NCAEP 2020 Evidence-Based Practice—relies on systematic, explicit error correction<sup>[50]</sup>, and **Agnoli (2021)** demonstrated that in multiple-choice testing for autism, immediately neutralizing/excluding the chosen wrong option on a 3-option MCQ and prompting the student to re-read and try once more prevents perseverative errors and captures true knowledge<sup>[40]</sup>.

## 7. Rewards and Interest-Based Themes (Minecraft-Style Themes)

This section requires careful distinction between **broad special-interest evidence** and **Minecraft-specific evidence**:

Moreover, **Boven (2018)** warns that if a special interest is attached to an academic task in a vague or tangential way, autistic students can become confused about relevance or pulled into off-task associations<sup>[77]</sup>. Therefore, using Minecraft as an *antecedent structural metaphor and token system* is grounded in strong ABI principles, but specific Minecraft UI styling rules are rated CONSENSUS or WEAK and must obey strict coherence limits<sup>[23]</sup>.

## 8. Read-Aloud (Text-to-Speech and Language-School Dual Modality)

For a Grade 6 autistic student in an Egyptian language school, read-aloud functionality intersects two critical cognitive mechanisms:

- **Compensatory Decoding Support & Bimodal Testing**: Meta-analyses (**Wood et al., 2018**, 22 studies, N = 2,942, mean weighted d = 0.35; **Li, 2014**; **Ryan, 2024**) confirm that read-aloud/Text-to-Speech (TTS) accommodations significantly improve reading comprehension for students with reading or decoding challenges, with natural human-like prosody outperforming robotic speech<sup>[20]</sup>. In autism assessment specifically, **Agnoli (2021)** and **Keelor et al. (2020)** emphasize providing bimodal (visual text + auditory read-aloud) access for every lesson passage and every MCQ/True-False item<sup>[40]</sup>. However, **NCEO research syntheses** note that students with strong decoding or hyperlexia may not benefit if forced to listen to slow audio<sup>[85]</sup>, and **Keelor et al. (2020)** found that in 8–11-year-olds with faster processing speed, **TTS** ***without*** **word-by-word highlighting** correlated positively with comprehension (r = 0.687, p < 0.05)<sup>[84]</sup>.

- **The Reverse Redundancy Effect for Second-Language (L2) Learners**: While Mayer's traditional Redundancy Principle advises against showing full on-screen text during audio narration for native-speaking adults<sup>[19]</sup>, empirical studies with **children** (**Knoop-van Campen et al., 2018**, demonstrating the *transient information effect* where fleeting spoken audio overloads working memory unless anchored by permanent written text)<sup>[87]</sup> and **non-native English (EFL/L2) learners** (**2025 Reverse Redundancy Study**, showing a significant comprehension gain of Cohen's d = 0.57, p < 0.05 when synchronized on-screen text and audio narration are presented together)<sup>[18]</sup> demonstrate that **written text should remain permanently visible on screen while read-aloud audio plays**.

## 9. Session Length and Breaks

Empirical studies of Computer-Assisted Instruction (CAI) with autistic children consistently indicate that **short, structured micro-sessions** prevent fatigue, preserve executive attention, and prevent obsessive screen fixation. In classroom CAI trials with autistic boys aged 7–10, **Pennington et al. (2012)** achieved rapid skill mastery and maintenance using sessions lasting **5 to 10 minutes**<sup>[89]</sup>. Similarly, in **Fletcher-Watson et al.'s (2016)** two-month trial (N = 41), average daily usage was **11 minutes per day**, which produced positive engagement with zero evidence of obsessive behavior<sup>[66]</sup>. Qualitative and quantitative studies of neurodivergent students in online learning (**PMC11020716, 2024**; **FEDIS+R Framework, 2024**) confirm that breaking lessons into short subsections with scheduled breaks and removing time pressure are essential for managing cognitive fatigue<sup>[88]</sup>.

## 10. Question Design for MCQ and True/False

Under **Egyptian MoETE Ministerial Decree No. 252 of 2017**, integrated students with autism in general and language schools are assessed **100% via objective questions** (principally Multiple-Choice and True/False) with questions and answer spaces integrated on the same sheet<sup>[3]</sup>. Designing these two item types requires combining **Accessibility Theory in Educational Measurement** (minimizing construct-irrelevant cognitive and linguistic variance, *Beddow, 2012; NCME Fifth Edition, 2026*)<sup>[93]</sup> with empirical item-writing taxonomies (*Haladyna, Downing, & Rodriguez, 2002; Rodriguez, 2005; Moreno, Martínez, & Muñiz, 2015; Rush et al., 2023*)<sup>[21]</sup>.

## 11. Avoiding Overload (Cognitive and Sensory Load Management)

Neurodivergent learners experience substantially higher **extraneous cognitive load** from digital interfaces than neurotypical peers, meaning that poor layout, competing visual elements, or deep navigation trees rapidly exhaust working memory before the student even processes the academic lesson (*PMC11020716, 2024; FEDIS+R Framework, 2024*)<sup>[88]</sup>. Crucially, a **2025/2026 experimental study on visual multimedia overload thresholds (PMC12775221)** demonstrated that for students learning in a second language (English) or having lower sustained attention, **presenting 3 or more visual elements on a single slide/screen crosses their cognitive overload threshold**, causing visuals to act as distractors that degrade global comprehension and recall<sup>[102]</sup>.

| **Rule** | **Strength** | **Source (with link and year)** | **How to check it on a page (a yes/no test)** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **OV1. ## "Check With His Inclusion Team" List (High Individual Variability)

Autism is characterized by high intra- and inter-individual heterogeneity across sensory, linguistic, and executive profiles (*Aguiar et al., 2022; Kalandadze et al., 2018; 2025 Palgrave Sensory Processing Study*)<sup>[46]</sup>. Rather than hard-coding rigid assumptions for the following eight areas, verify each parameter directly with the student, his parents, and his school's inclusion division (*قسم* *الدمج* */* *غرفة* *المصادر*) before finalizing his profile settings:

- **Exact Option Count on His School's Official Inclusion Exams (3 vs. 4 MCQ Options):** While psychometric research shows 3 MCQ options (A, B, C) are optimal<sup>[21]</sup>, Egyptian educational directorates (*الإدارات* *التعليمية*) and individual language schools issue specific templates under Ministerial Decree No. 252 of 2017<sup>[3]</sup>. Check his recent school inclusion exam papers to verify whether his teachers use 3 options or 4 options on Grade 6 exams, and match that exact number on his practice quizzes so exam familiarity is preserved<sup>[36]</sup>.

- **Bilingual Scaffolding & Arabic Glossary Support:** Because he attends an Egyptian language school, determine which subjects are examined in English (e.g., Math, Science, English) versus Arabic (e.g., Arabic Language, Social Studies, Religion), and confirm whether he is exempt from the Second Foreign Language under Article 10 of Decree 252/2017<sup>[3]</sup>. Ask his inclusion teacher whether providing a toggleable Arabic translation for new English technical terms in the "Key Words" box helps bridge comprehension or whether he studies more comfortably in English alone<sup>[14]</sup>.

- **Sensory Colour Profile (Muted Earthy Light Mode vs. Soft Dark Mode vs. Specific Tints):** While most autistic boys with sensory hypersensitivity prefer muted greens, browns, and soft cream/gray backgrounds and dislike bright yellow<sup>[38]</sup>, a 2025 sensory-processing study confirms that sensory-seeking autistic learners sometimes prefer bolder colours or dark mode, and individual overlay preferences vary widely<sup>[59]</sup>. Test the 3 calm background presets with him directly and let him choose his default<sup>[14]</sup>.

- **Read-Aloud (TTS) Default State, Voice, Speed, and Highlighting Granularity:** Students with autism vary from hyperlexic (rapid visual decoders who find slow spoken audio distracting) to struggling decoders who rely on TTS<sup>[84]</sup>. Check with his inclusion team whether he uses a human reader/legal companion (*مرافق* *تربوي* */* *قانوني*) during school exams under Decree 252/2017<sup>[3]</sup>, whether he prefers 1.0x or 1.25x audio speed, and whether word-by-word highlighting helps him track or bothers his eyes<sup>[84]</sup>.

- **Minecraft Interest Boundaries (Engaging Scaffold vs. Perseverative Distraction):** Ask his inclusion specialist and parents *how* he engages with Minecraft (e.g., Creative Mode building, Redstone logic circuits, or Survival crafting) and whether seeing Minecraft item icons inside a lesson keeps him regulated and motivated<sup>[25]</sup> or triggers off-task scripting/daydreaming about the game<sup>[77]</sup>. If Minecraft analogies in lesson text distract him, restrict the Minecraft theme strictly to the top progress bar and end-of-lesson reward screen (consequence-based token system)<sup>[66]</sup>.

- **Error-Handling Tolerance (Guided Error Correction vs. Immediate Hint / Errorless Fading):** Although high-functioning autistic learners generally achieve higher independent mastery with explicit error correction and a second attempt (**Leaf et al., 2020**)<sup>[72]</sup>, some autistic students experience acute perfectionism or distress when an answer is marked incorrect<sup>[70]</sup>. Ask his resource-room teacher how he reacts to mistakes; if errors trigger shutdowns, enable an optional "Show Clue First" prompt button (errorless scaffolding) before he locks in his answer<sup>[112]</sup>.

- **Optimal Micro-Session Duration and Break Activity:** Confirm whether his sustained independent attention span in Grade 6 is closer to **5–10 minutes** (3 lesson cards + 3 questions) or **15–20 minutes** (5 lesson cards + 6 questions), and ask his occupational therapist or inclusion specialist what specific 2-minute break activity (e.g., stretching, drinking water, or a calm visual breathing/block-stacking pause) regulates him best between modules<sup>[88]</sup>.

- **Input Modality (Touchscreen Tablet vs. Desktop Mouse/Keyboard):** Confirm whether he studies on an iPad/tablet or a desktop/laptop computer so touch-sensitivity debouncing or keyboard shortcut navigation (1, 2, 3 for options, Enter to check) can be tailored to his fine-motor profile<sup>[14]</sup>.

## Combined Yes/No Page-Review Checklist

Use this single consolidated checklist to audit every lesson and quiz web page before publishing it for the student. Every item must pass with a **YES**.

### A. Layout and Predictability

- [ ] **L1 (Single-Column Micro-Task):** Is all content arranged in a single vertical column displaying only one concept card or one quiz question per view, requiring at most one screen-height of vertical scrolling and zero horizontal scrolling?<sup>[16]</sup>
- [ ] **L2 (Explicit Container Borders):** Does every functional section on the page (header, lesson box, audio toolbar, quiz card) have a visible enclosing border (1px–2px solid line) separated by at least 16px–24px of whitespace?<sup>[1]</sup>
- [ ] **L3 (Identical Control Placement):** Do the page title, progress bar, Read-Aloud bar, Back button, and primary action button stay in the exact same screen coordinates and visual style across every page?<sup>[1]</sup>
- [ ] **L4 (Descriptive Action Buttons):** Does every button include both a standard icon and explicit text stating exactly what will happen when clicked (e.g., "Check My Answer", "Go to Quiz Question 2")?<sup>[1]</sup>
- [ ] **L5 (18pt Ragged-Right Typography):** Is all body and question text set in a clean sans-serif font at 24px (18pt, matching Egypt's MoETE inclusion standard) or larger, with ragged-right alignment, ≥1.5 line height, zero hyphenation, and no italics or ALL CAPS?<sup>[4]</sup>
- [ ] **L6 (Large Clickable Option Cards):** Is the entire bordered container of each MCQ or True/False option clickable (at least 44px tall with a clear hover/focus outline) rather than just a small radio button?<sup>[11]</sup>

### B. Language (Literal, Short)

- [ ] **LG1 (100% Literal Language):** Can every sentence on the page be interpreted word-for-word literally, with zero unexplained idioms, metaphors, irony, or figures of speech?<sup>[1]</sup>
- [ ] **LG2 (Short Bulleted Sentences):** Are explanations broken into bullet points or short 2–3 sentence blocks where every sentence is 20 words or fewer (and ideally ≤15 words)?<sup>[1]</sup>
- [ ] **LG3 (Active Voice & Positive Syntax):** Is every sentence written in the active voice using simple tense, with zero double negatives or nested relative clauses?<sup>[1]</sup>
- [ ] **LG4 (Pre-Taught Key Vocabulary):** Are all new curriculum terms and abbreviations defined in a visible "Key Words" box at the start of the lesson or via an inline glossary on the same screen?<sup>[11]</sup>
- [ ] **LG5 (Digits for Numbers):** Are all numbers (including numbers under 10) written as Arabic numerals (1, 2, 3) with zero Roman numerals (I, II, III)?<sup>[1]</sup>

### C. Visuals and Dual Coding

- [ ] **V1 (Instructional Visual Pairing):** Does every core lesson card pair its text with a directly relevant diagram, concept map, or instructional illustration?<sup>[24]</sup>
- [ ] **V2 (Side-by-Side Spatial Contiguity):** Is the instructional image placed immediately beside its matching text (image on left, text on right for English) with labels attached directly to diagram parts rather than in a separate legend?<sup>[19]</sup>
- [ ] **V3 (Clean, Uncluttered Visual Fidelity):** Is every image in sharp focus on a plain or isolated background, free of complex background scenes or overlapping semi-transparent layers?<sup>[39]</sup>
- [ ] **V4 (Visual Signaling Cues):** Are the 1–3 critical features of the diagram or concept highlighted with arrows, numbered badges, or callout borders?<sup>[14]</sup>
- [ ] **V5 (Zero Decorative Graphics or Hero Banners):** Is the page completely free of purely decorative clip art, stock photos of faces looking at the camera, and large top banners above the fold?<sup>[19]</sup>

### D. Colour

- [ ] **C1 (Muted Earthy Palette, No Bright Yellow):** Does the page use a muted, low-saturation palette (soft greens, browns, slate blues, grays) with zero neon colours and zero bright yellow backgrounds?<sup>[17]</sup>
- [ ] **C2 (Soft Non-Glare Contrast ≥4.5:1):** Is body text dark charcoal on a soft off-white/cream/sage background (avoiding pure #FFFFFF glare) with a verified contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1?<sup>[17]</sup>
- [ ] **C3 (Redundant Non-Colour Cues):** Are all colour-coded states (correct, hint, key word, primary action) paired with a distinct icon, border style, or text label so they remain 100% clear in grayscale?<sup>[1]</sup>
- [ ] **C4 (Background Tint Switcher):** Is there a simple one-click control allowing the student to switch between 2–3 calm background tint presets?<sup>[14]</sup>

### E. Motion and Sound

- [ ] **MS1 (Zero Auto-Play Audio):** Is the page 100% silent on load and during scrolling, playing audio only when the student explicitly clicks a "Listen" button?<sup>[11]</sup>
- [ ] **MS2 (Zero Autonomous Motion):** Is the screen completely free of blinking elements, looping GIFs, moving text, parallax scrolling, and animated loading screens, while respecting prefers-reduced-motion?<sup>[11]</sup>
- [ ] **MS3 (User-Stepped Process Animations):** If an instructional animation is present, is it controlled entirely by the user via step-by-step ("Step 1", "Step 2") or Play/Pause buttons?<sup>[19]</sup>
- [ ] **MS4 (Instant <100 ms Debounced Response):** Does clicking any button or option produce an immediate visual highlight with zero lag and protection against accidental double-clicks?<sup>[35]</sup>

### F. Feedback After Wrong Answers

- [ ] **FB1 (Non-Punitive, No Error Spectacle):** When a wrong answer is submitted, does the page avoid buzzers, red flashes, sad faces, point deductions, and entertaining error animations?<sup>[42]</sup>
- [ ] **FB2 (Action-Oriented Corrective Hint):** Does a wrong answer immediately display a calm, specific explanation pointing to the exact lesson rule or clue rather than just saying "Incorrect"?<sup>[11]</sup>
- [ ] **FB3 (Option Exclusion on MCQ / Immediate Worked Solution on True-False):** On a 3-option MCQ, does a wrong choice grey out/disable that option and allow one guided retry; and on a 2-option True/False item, does it immediately show the worked explanation without allowing a blind 50/50 guess?<sup>[40]</sup>
- [ ] **FB4 (In-Place Lesson Review):** Does the feedback box include a one-click "Review Lesson Block" button or panel that shows the relevant lesson excerpt without losing quiz progress?<sup>[1]</sup>

### G. Rewards and Interest-Based Themes (Minecraft-Style)

- [ ] **RW1 (Explicit Antecedent Interest Link):** If a Minecraft analogy is used in a lesson example, does the first sentence explicitly state how it connects to the school curriculum term?<sup>[25]</sup>
- [ ] **RW2 (Predictable Token Progress Bar):** Does the top bar award a visible crafting block/token for each completed lesson card and quiz item, leading to a calm completion summary at the end of the unit?<sup>[24]</sup>
- [ ] **RW3 (Clean Structural Theme Boundaries):** Is the Minecraft styling strictly confined to borders, progress badges, and muted earthy colours—with zero pixel fonts for reading text and zero game textures behind text?<sup>[19]</sup>
- [ ] **RW4 (Mastery Focus Over Leaderboards):** Does the reward system celebrate concept mastery and unit completion without competitive leaderboards, streak-loss penalties, or random loot mechanics?<sup>[14]</sup>

### H. Read-Aloud (Text-to-Speech)

- [ ] **RA1 (Click-to-Speak on All Text & Items):** Does every lesson card, question stem, and answer option have an on-demand "Listen" button?<sup>[20]</sup>
- [ ] **RA2 (Permanent On-Screen Text During Audio):** Does the full written text remain permanently visible on the screen while the Read-Aloud audio plays?<sup>[18]</sup>
- [ ] **RA3 (Natural Voice with Speed Control):** Does the audio player use a calm human recording or high-naturalness neural voice with working Pause, Replay, and speed (0.75x–1.5x) controls?<sup>[20]</sup>
- [ ] **RA4 (Toggleable Text Highlighting):** Can synchronized text highlighting during Read-Aloud be toggled on or off by the user?<sup>[20]</sup>

### I. Session Length and Breaks

- [ ] **SL1 (5–15 Minute Micro-Module Scope):** Is the unit scoped so that reading the lesson cards and answering the quiz takes approximately 5 to 15 minutes?<sup>[19]</sup>
- [ ] **SL2 (Upfront Visual Roadmap):** Does the module start with a clear summary of how many lesson blocks and quiz questions are included, with a "Step X of Y" counter on every screen?<sup>[1]</sup>
- [ ] **SL3 (100% Self-Paced, Zero Timers):** Is the page completely free of countdown clocks, timed lockouts, and speed-based scoring?<sup>[1]</sup>
- [ ] **SL4 (Auto-Save & Explicit Break Checkpoints):** Does the page auto-save progress after every step and present an explicit "Take a Break / Continue" checkpoint between the lesson and the quiz?<sup>[1]</sup>

### J. Question Design for MCQ and True/False

- [ ] **Q1 (Segmented Same-Screen Assessment):** Are quiz questions placed immediately after their relevant lesson segment with answer controls integrated inside the same question container?<sup>[1]</sup>
- [ ] **Q2 (Behavioral Momentum Sequencing):** Does the quiz begin with 1–2 straightforward, high-probability recall items before introducing harder application items?<sup>[14]</sup>
- [ ] **Q3 (Positive, Complete Question Stems):** Is every MCQ stem written as a complete, positively phrased direct question ending in a ? (no fill-in-the-blank stems and no NOT/EXCEPT stems)?<sup>[1]</sup>
- [ ] **Q4 (3 Homogeneous Vertical Options, No Meta-Options):** Does every MCQ present 3 (or school-mandated 4) plausible options of similar length stacked vertically in logical order, with zero "All of the above" or "None of the above" choices?<sup>[21]</sup>
- [ ] **Q5 (Single-Premise Affirmative True/False):** Does every True/False statement test only one single, positive factual premise (zero compound two-clause statements and zero negative words like "not")?<sup>[1]</sup>
- [ ] **Q6 (Zero Absolute or Vague Qualifiers):** Are all MCQ and True/False items completely free of always, never, all, none, sometimes, often, and usually?<sup>[43]</sup>
- [ ] **Q7 (Explicit Input Prompt & Confirmation Button):** Does every question display "Choose one option:" above the choices and require clicking "Check My Answer" to submit?<sup>[1]</sup>

### K. Avoiding Overload

- [ ] **OV1 (Maximum 1–2 Visuals Per Screen):** Does the screen display no more than 1–2 instructional images or diagrams at a time?<sup>[39]</sup>
- [ ] **OV2 (No Rote Memory Across Screens):** Are all needed reference facts, formulas, key words, or diagrams visible or expandable right on the quiz screen?<sup>[1]</sup>
- [ ] **OV3 (≤3-Click Flat Navigation, No Hover/Pop-Ups):** Can the page be reached within 3 clicks from the home screen without any hover-triggered menus, pop-up modals, or floating widgets?<sup>[1]</sup>
- [ ] **OV4 (Quiet Background Educator Log):** Are the student's question attempts and error patterns logged quietly to a separate teacher/parent history view while keeping the student's workspace simple?<sup>[42]</sup>
